Your RL Sampler Is 50 to 150 Steps Behind: bf16 Export Rounding and the Fair Cast Fix

2 November 2026 · Anpoo Sivanadi

Most RL post-training stacks share one seam. The optimizer holds fp32 master weights, an inference engine such as vLLM or SGLang holds a bf16 copy and generates the rollouts, and after each optimizer step something like master.to(torch.bfloat16) pushes the new weights across. Most published mismatch work targets kernel-level differences between trainer and sampler. A new paper argues the bigger problem is the cast itself.

"bf16 Rounding Silently Discards Most RL Policy Updates: A Hidden Training-Inference Mismatch and an Unbiased One-Line Fix" (Andrew Gao and William Gao, Stanford, arXiv 2610.41073, submitted Oct 29) measures what round-to-nearest export does to learning. On Qwen3-1.7B only 0.4 to 0.5% of served weights change per optimizer step, and the policy generating the training data lags the optimizer by 50 to 150 steps. The fix is a change at the export call plus training on the served copy, and the code is public at andrewgcodes/faircast.

1. The dead zone

bf16 keeps a 7-bit significand, so near a weight of magnitude |w| the grid spacing is about |w|/128. Round-to-nearest maps any update smaller than half that spacing, about |w|/256, to zero. The fp32 master starts exactly on the bf16 grid, so this applies from step one.

At the learning rates the paper calls common for RL post-training (AdamW at 1e-6), almost every Adam update falls inside that band. A drifting weight crosses a rounding boundary only every 50 to 200 steps, and under a decaying learning-rate schedule the sampler can effectively freeze while the optimizer keeps moving.

A single-step experiment makes it concrete. On 64 knights-and-knaves problems, one optimizer step at 1e-6 moved an fp32 sampler from 0.17 to 0.84 accuracy. The round-to-nearest bf16 sampler stayed at 0.19 across three steps.

Muon is worse: its nearly uniform per-element updates almost all sit below the threshold, leaving the sampler close to frozen for the first 20 steps.

2. Why your mismatch metrics stay green

Frameworks correct for trainer-versus-sampler mismatch with PPO clipping, truncated importance sampling (TIS) or IcePop masking. All of those compare the trainer's log-probabilities with the sampler's. If the trainer's forward pass runs in bf16, both sides are computed from the same rounded copy, so the ratio sits near one no matter how far the fp32 master has moved.

The authors logged both ratios. The visible trainer-versus-sampler ratio stayed at a mean per-token |log ρ| of about 0.01 for the whole run. The hidden master-versus-served ratio grew to 0.10 to 0.16 on MATH and about 0.45 on knights-and-knaves. Per-token effective sample size between master and served policy fell to 0.2 to 0.35 on tasks with 2,000-token responses.

The paper says every open-source framework it examined (verl, TRL, OpenRLHF, slime, prime-rl, AReaL) produces the served copy with round-to-nearest. It also notes that lowering the learning rate, which prior work recommended for mismatch, reduces the visible ratio while increasing the hidden one.

3. Fair Cast: two changes, both required

  1. Export with an unbiased rounding rule. Stochastic rounding (sr_cast_bf16) adds 16 random bits to the fp32 bit pattern and truncates, so the served weight equals the master in expectation. Error feedback (ef_cast_bf16) carries a per-weight fp32 residual so accumulated export error stays bounded.
  2. Compute the loss on the served copy. The trainer's forward pass uses the exact bf16 weights that produced the rollouts, with gradients passed to the fp32 master through a straight-through estimator.

The first half alone is harmful. Error-feedback export without training on the served copy collapsed to 0.00 held-out accuracy in 4 of 4 long runs. Stochastic-rounding export alone degenerated on Qwen3-0.6B, with GSM8K falling from 0.61 to 0.13 by step 19 and MATH to 0.002. The second half alone changes nothing, since both copies still sit downstream of the same rounding.

The cost is small. On an H100, a full export of Qwen3-1.7B takes 4.3 ms with round-to-nearest, 21.7 ms with stochastic rounding and 37.2 ms with error feedback, which also needs 6.9 GB of fp32 residual. Optimizer steps in their runs took 31 to 130 s, so the export is below 0.1% of the step.

4. The results

Runs use GRPO-style clipped policy gradient, group size 8, 24 prompts per step, 150 optimizer steps, one H100 per arm with a co-located vLLM sampler. Selected endpoints from the paper's Table 2 (Qwen3-1.7B with AdamW unless noted):

Task Model Round-to-nearest Fair Cast Δ (points)
Zebra puzzles 1.7B 0.33 ± 0.10 0.57 ± 0.16 +24
Knights-and-knaves 0.6B 0.865 0.967 +10.2
Knights-and-knaves, Muon 1.7B 0.907 ± 0.016 0.988 ± 0.003 +8.2
Knights-and-knaves 4B 0.988 0.992 +0.4
GSM8K 0.6B 0.713 ± 0.025 0.764 ± 0.024 +5.1
MATH levels 3 to 5 0.6B 0.506 ± 0.025 0.533 ± 0.015 +2.7
MBPP+ (224 held-out) 1.7B 0.595 ± 0.007 0.618 ± 0.017 +2.3
MATH levels 3 to 5 1.7B 0.664 0.656 -0.8

Across 25 matched seed pairs, Fair Cast is ahead by more than a point on 18, within a point on 6 and behind on 1, with a mean gain of 6.1 points. The repo's pairs table also gives a median of +3.1. The three zebra pairs (+35.5, +17.8, +43.6) pull the mean up. My read: the median is the fairer headline for most tasks, and zebra is the real story for long rollouts.

On zebra, round-to-nearest learned for about 20 steps, then responses lengthened into the 2,048-token cap (held-out truncation reached 0.47 to 0.89) while training reward fell. A DAPO-style truncation penalty did not prevent the collapse. Three of four Fair Cast runs ended at 0.62 to 0.70 and tracked an fp32-sampler oracle at 0.69.

5. The checkpoint is not the model

Under round-to-nearest, the saved fp32 master and the served bf16 copy drift apart because the master holds updates the sampler never saw. On HumanEval+ the served copy scored 59.8 while its own fp32 master scored 9.1 at step 99. Across nine round-to-nearest runs with master evaluations, five showed a gap of at least 12 points at some checkpoint.

The export format decides which model you get:

Same saved master, exported as bf16 fp16 FP8 via bf16 FP8 via fp16
HumanEval+, round-to-nearest 58.5 16.5 60.4 28.1
Countdown (500 steps), round-to-nearest 21.9 10.2 20.3 10.2
HumanEval+, Fair Cast 64.6 62.2 61.0 61.0

Formats at or coarser than bf16 reproduce the training-time score; anything finer, including resuming from the fp32 master, inherits the drift. The fp16 export of that HumanEval+ master averaged 1,679-token responses against 344 for the bf16 export. Under Fair Cast, master and export agreed within 3 points at every checkpoint across 12 runs.

6. Where it hurts, and the limits

Round-to-nearest acts as an accidental low-pass filter on the policy, and on one task that helped. On list functions, Fair Cast without lag collapsed to bare answers by step 29 and plateaued at 0.523, against 0.668 for round-to-nearest. Exporting an EMA of the master with α = 0.05, still with unbiased rounding, reached 0.707 and 0.709 on two seeds. The lag becomes a hyperparameter instead of a side effect of the number format.

Other limits before you quote this:

  • Small models. Everything is 4B parameters or below, on one training implementation, and several configurations have a single seed. The authors put the standard error of one endpoint difference at about 3 points on 512 problems and 6 on 128.
  • Gains shrink near ceiling. Knights-and-knaves at 4B gained 0.4 points; OLMoE-1B-7B and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct were within noise.
  • Delta sync gets less sparse. Fair Cast changes 2 to 15% of served weights per step instead of 0.4 to 0.5%, so schemes that ship only changed bf16 elements, like TRL's delta weight sync, compress 5 to 30x less.
  • Large-scale behavior is open. My read, not the paper's: I'd most want this rerun on large MoE RL with FP8 serving, since the dead-zone math depends on weight magnitudes and learning rate.

7. What I'd check in my own RL loop

  1. Log the hidden ratio. Every few steps, evaluate log-probabilities from the fp32 master on a batch of rollouts and compare with the sampler's. If that grows while the trainer-versus-sampler ratio stays near 0.01, you have this problem.
  2. Count changed served weights per sync. The paper measured 0.4 to 0.5% under round-to-nearest; numbers like that mean the sampler is mostly not moving.
  3. Try stochastic rounding at the export, with the served-copy forward. Never use the unbiased export without the served-copy forward; that combination collapsed.
  4. Evaluate and deploy what was served. Score the bf16 served copy, not the fp32 checkpoint, and don't release an fp16 model or resume from the fp32 master after round-to-nearest training without re-evaluating.
  5. Treat Muon and low-LR runs as high risk. They sit deepest in the dead zone.

I like this paper because it explains a class of "RL just plateaued" failures with arithmetic you can check in a notebook, and the fix costs about 20 ms per sync. The evidence is small-model, but the step 1 diagnostic is cheap enough to run on any RL loop before deciding whether the fix matters at your scale.
