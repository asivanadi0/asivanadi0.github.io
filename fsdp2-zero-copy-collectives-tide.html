FSDP2 Pays for More Than Wire Time: Reading the TIDE Collectives Paper

1 November 2026 · Anpoo Sivanadi

When an FSDP2 job underperforms, the first thing most of us check is whether all-gather and reduce-scatter are hitting link bandwidth. If NCCL looks close to wire speed, we move on. A new paper from KlingAI Research argues that this check misses most of the cost: "The Endgame of FSDP Communication: Layout-Aware Collectives on Copy Engines, RDMA, and NVSwitch" (arXiv 2610.38228, submitted Oct 28).

Their system, TIDE, replaces FSDP2's all-gather and reduce-scatter with collectives built on public NCCL 2.31+ APIs. On 64 GPUs it cuts step time by up to 27.1% against stock FSDP2 and 6.0% against FSDP2 with PyTorch Symmetric Memory. The headline matters less to me than where the time was going in the first place, because those costs exist in any FSDP2 job I run today, with or without TIDE.

1. Three costs that are not wire time

The paper splits FSDP2's communication overhead into three parts.

Layout conversion. FSDP2 keeps parameters and gradients as per-parameter DTensor shards, but NCCL wants flat, rank-major buffers. So every all-gather output gets copied into parameter storage, and every group's bf16 gradients get packed into a temporary full-group fp32 buffer before reduce-scatter. On Llama-3.1 70B at 8K tokens per GPU, those copies take 0.33 s per step, which the paper puts at 6.4% of step time. My own arithmetic: that implies a step of roughly 5.2 s at that setting (estimate).

SM wave quantization. This was the part I found most useful. Big TMA GEMMs use over 200 KB of shared memory per thread block, so only one block fits per SM, and they launch as thread-block clusters. The paper works through a 256 x 8192 x 8192 GEMM, the kind of shape you get in MoE experts or narrow projections at small token counts. It forms 32 four-CTA clusters and the GPU has 33 slots for them, so it runs in exactly one wave. NCCL 2.31's default 32 MiB reduce-scatter on 8 nodes runs 16 CTAs in four clusters, takes three of those slots, and pushes 12 GEMM CTAs into a second wave. The GEMM slows by 69%.

HBM direction contention. Per byte served, remote reads hurt a co-running memory-bound kernel 2.2 to 4x more than inbound copy-engine writes. NVSwitch switch reductions are reads.

2. The reduce-scatter tradeoff, in one table

The obvious fix for wave quantization is to cap NCCL's CTA count. The paper's numbers show why that only moves the cost:

32 MiB reduce-scatter, 8 nodes Reduce-scatter time Slowdown of the one-wave GEMM
NCCL default (16 CTAs, 4-CTA clusters) 6.75 ms 69.0%
NCCL capped at 8 CTAs not given 67.8%
NCCL capped at 4 CTAs 26.35 ms 2.7%
NCCL, 8 CTAs, cluster size 1 13.0 ms 6.3%
TIDE training config (16 sum + 4 landing CTAs) 6.96 ms 2.9%

You either get a fast reduce-scatter that wrecks the overlapping GEMM, or a polite one that takes four times as long. Stream priority doesn't help, the authors point out, because CUDA does not preempt resident CTAs. And a grouped GEMM that fills every SM, like the Qwen3-30B-A3B experts (128 groups of 512 rows), has no spare slots at all: every NCCL and symmetric-memory reduce-scatter they measured slowed it by about 70%, against about 5% for TIDE.

The HBM direction numbers are just as concrete. With GPUs 1 to 7 of a node driving traffic at GPU 0 while it runs a 256 MiB memory-bound kernel:

Initiator Pull: served GB/s, victim slowdown Push: served GB/s, victim slowdown
SM 788, 46.7% 721, 12.1%
Copy engine 838, 43.9% 798, 11.0%

Normalized, pulls cost 5.2 to 5.9% per 100 GB/s and pushes 1.4 to 1.7%. A 2-CTA switch-reduction collective slowed the same victim by 27%; TIDE's reduce-scatter by 2.6%.

3. What TIDE does differently

The idea is that layout conversion is a collective problem, not a copy problem. If the collective reads each tensor where the model stores it and writes each result to its final address, the copies and the packing buffers go away.

  • All-gather (TIDE-AG): copy engines multicast shards through NVSwitch inside a node, and paced recursive-doubling RDMA carries them across nodes, landing directly in parameter storage. It uses no SMs.
  • Reduce-scatter (TIDE-RS): copy engines push unreduced bf16 gradients (half the bytes, and the cheap push direction) into small staging chunks, 4 to 16 short-lived TMA CTAs sum the 8-to-1 node reduction in fp32, and then each node sends one fp32 partial per element over rail RDMA. A Latin-square placement lets the destination NVSwitch fold up to 8 nodes' partials per wave with multimem.ld_reduce.add.v4.f32.
  • fp32 semantics kept. Reducing bf16 gradients into fp32 shards is, per the paper, a mixed-precision mode NCCL does not provide. They also explain why a bf16 symmetric window doesn't work: the acc::f32.bf16x2 form accumulates in fp32 but returns bf16, so node partials get rounded before cross-node accumulation.

The CTAs it does use are short-lived, about 0.2 to 0.4 ms each, resident for roughly a third of a reduce-scatter. That is the whole trick against wave quantization: residence time, not just CTA count.

4. Results on 64 GPUs

All training runs use 8 nodes of 8 GPUs per NVSwitch domain, one RDMA rail per GPU, median step time over 40 to 100 post-warm-up steps.

Model and tokens/GPU Step-time cut vs stock FSDP2 vs FSDP2+SymmMem
Qwen3-30B-A3B, 16K / 24K / 32K 27.1% / 21.4% / 17.3% 6.0% / 4.8% / 4.3%
Llama-3.1 70B, 8K / 32K 16.2% / 3.1% 1.9 to 2.4x SymmMem's saving from 16K to 32K
Wan2.2 14B expert, 7.8K / 15.6K / 31.2K 7.4% / 2.8% / 1.4% about 2x SymmMem's saving at the two larger sizes

The MoE result is the expected one. Communication overhead scales with sharded parameter bytes, while useful compute scales with activated parameters times tokens. Sparse routing makes the recoverable overhead a bigger share of the step. On dense Llama the absolute saving stays at 0.51 to 0.54 s per step from 16K to 32K tokens, so the percentage shrinks only because the step grows.

Other numbers worth keeping:

  • Communication SM time on a Llama 8K step dropped from 78 SM-seconds (stock) and 34 (SymmMem) to 3.8. A communication kernel was resident for 8% of the step instead of 89% and 59%.
  • TIDE-AG is 1.06 to 1.42x faster than FSDP2's fastest all-gather plus copy-out, growing from 1.13x at 16 GPUs to 1.42x at 64 on 128 MiB shards.
  • FSDP2's reduce-scatter buffer doubles with GPU count, reaching 2.03 and 8.12 GiB at 64 GPUs; TIDE's stays near 0.6 and 2.4 GiB, a 3.2x gap.
  • Peak memory is up to 4.7 GiB below FSDP2+SymmMem. On Qwen3 at 32K: 87.3 vs 91.0 GiB, within 0.3 GiB of stock. Its in-place mode does add 1.9 GiB over stock on Llama at 24K tokens.
  • Loss on a 1,000-step Qwen3-30B-A3B run on C4 stayed within stock's own run-to-run spread: 0.73% max deviation for TIDE against 1.12% between two stock runs.

5. Limits before you get excited

  • No code yet. The authors "intend to release" the implementation, benchmark drivers and correctness gate on publication.
  • Narrow contract. PyTorch 2.13, dimension-0 sharding, up to 8 GPUs per NVSwitch domain, rail-aligned RDMA NICs, NVSwitch multicast, compute capability 9.0 or higher with at least 207 KB of shared memory per block. No CUDA-graph capture or async unshard yet. Groups with more than 256 trainable parameters fall back to stock collectives.
  • One cluster shape. All results are 64 GPUs. The paper describes 148 SMs per GPU but doesn't name the part in what I read; 148 SMs matches a B200-class GPU, which is my inference, not the paper's statement.
  • Related work is moving. NVIDIA's PACE in NCCL contrib already fuses layout conversion and casting into AG/RS kernels on NCCL's GIN device API, and AMD has an open PyTorch RFC for SDMA all-gather with zero-copy output. The paper positions TIDE as the one that keeps per-parameter storage, fp32 reduction and off-SM transport all at once.

6. What I'd check on my own jobs

  1. Profile for copies, not just collectives. Look for the all-gather copy-out and _chunk_cat style packing kernels in an Nsight trace and add up their time per step. If it's near 6% on a dense model, that's real money.
  2. Measure GEMM slowdown under overlap. Time your hottest small-M expert or projection GEMM alone, then with a reduce-scatter running beside it. A near-2x jump means wave quantization.
  3. Try cluster size 1 before capping CTAs. In the paper, 8 single CTAs cut the GEMM penalty from 69% to 6.3% while costing less reduce-scatter time than a 4-CTA cap.
  4. Watch reduce-scatter buffer growth as you add nodes. If it doubles with GPU count at fixed shard size, that memory is coming out of your activation budget.
  5. Expect the biggest wins on sparse MoE at modest tokens per GPU. That's where sharded bytes are largest relative to activated compute.

I wrote about the FSDP capacity tax on MoE in my Olmo-core 3 note. This paper is the runtime side: even when the wire is fast, the GPU is paying for layout copies, displaced GEMM waves and the wrong HBM direction. Until code lands I can't verify the numbers, but the measurement method alone is worth stealing.
