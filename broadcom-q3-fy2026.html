Broadcom’s Q3: Custom ASICs, Gigawatts, and Why Near-Term Guidance Got Sold

3 September 2026 · Anpoo Sivanadi, Staff Software Engineer

Custom ASICs for frontier labs, gigawatt deployment targets, and why a beat with soft near-term guidance got sold.

I build platforms for teams that need to ship with confidence. When a semiconductor company reports that it expects to double AI revenue twice in two years while guiding the next quarter light, I do not read that as a vibe. I read it as a stack diagram: who designs the silicon, who buys the watts, and which constraint actually decides whether those watts become shipped systems.

Broadcom’s fiscal Q3 FY2026 print landed on September 2. The headline numbers cleared the Street. Adjusted earnings came in at $3.32 per share on $29.59 billion in sales, up 86% from $15.95 billion a year earlier, against consensus around $3.24 and $29.36 billion. Net income more than tripled to $13.09 billion. Semiconductor revenue roughly tripled year over year to $16.7 billion, beating the $15.2 billion target; infrastructure software contributed $8.75 billion, a hair under the $8.82 billion consensus. Market capitalization sat north of $1.7 trillion after a multi-year climb. Then after-hours trading sold the stock about 2% because fourth-quarter revenue guidance of roughly $34.8 billion at the midpoint trailed the Street’s $35.03 billion. That gap is small in absolute dollars and large in what it signals about how investors now price AI infrastructure: demand talk is table stakes; conversion and timing are the trade.

What Hock Tan actually said on the call

The useful part of the call was not the beat. It was the customer map.

Broadcom is not competing with Nvidia by shipping a general-purpose GPU family. It is designing and shipping custom AI accelerators with the labs that want their own silicon. During the quarter Broadcom disclosed Jalapeno, a custom processor developed with OpenAI. On the call, CEO Hock Tan said the company wants to accelerate delivery of Google’s newest Ironwood tensor processing units both to Google and to Anthropic. He expects “tens of billions of dollars worth of processors” to Google each year for several years. Meta’s custom MTIA accelerator, optimized for inference and recommendation at scale, is moving into production shipments. That is a short list of names (Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta) covering a large fraction of frontier training and inference spend.

The capacity language was in gigawatts, not just dollars. Tan said Anthropic is expected to deploy about 5 gigawatts of Google’s TPU8i processors next year, with line of sight to another 10 gigawatts later. OpenAI is preparing to tape out a second-generation Jalapeno while already working concepts for a third; for 2027 Broadcom expects about 1.3 gigawatts of Jalapeno deployments, with line of sight above 5 gigawatts after that. If you operate clusters for a living, gigawatts are a planning unit: power, cooling, networking, and delivery schedules all have to line up before a rack becomes useful. Naming GW targets on an earnings call is Broadcom telling the Street that custom ASIC programs are now infrastructure programs, not science projects.

Forward guidance on the AI line was aggressive. Tan said he expects AI revenue to double over the next year to $115 billion, then double again to $230 billion the year after, with a path toward $30 of earnings per share in fiscal 2027. CFO Amie Thuener framed part of the relationship with the leading labs as helping bridge “the gap between their current cash flow and the significant upfront investments required for their businesses.” Tan went further: Broadcom is considering residual value guarantees (contingent liabilities) for two of those labs, because “it makes economic sense for Broadcom to invest in and enable these guys.” That is not a throwaway line. A residual value guarantee means Broadcom is willing to underwrite part of the economic risk of the silicon it ships into cash-constrained but capacity-hungry customers. Structurally, that pulls a chip vendor closer to a financing partner for AI buildouts.

Primary write-up of the numbers and call color: SiliconANGLE, Sep 2, 2026 (https://siliconangle.com/2026/09/02/broadcom-beats-expectations-as-ai-labs-double-down-on-custom-chips/).

Why I care as an engineer

I do not manage Broadcom’s P&L. I do care about what custom silicon does to the systems I build on.

First, the inference and training stacks are bifurcating. Nvidia still owns a large share of general GPU capacity, but the largest labs are funding ASIC roadmaps with Broadcom (and others) so that model architecture, memory hierarchy, and interconnect can co-evolve with the silicon. When OpenAI is already talking third-generation Jalapeno concepts while second-gen tapes out, the feedback loop between model teams and chip teams is measured in quarters, not product generations. If you are designing serving systems, you should assume that “the GPU” is no longer a single abstraction. You will see Google TPU Ironwood paths, OpenAI Jalapeno paths, Meta MTIA paths, and Nvidia paths with different batching, memory, and networking assumptions.

Second, gigawatt deployment targets force the rest of the stack into the critical path. Power delivery, liquid cooling, rack networking, and optical interconnect do not appear on Broadcom’s revenue line the same way, but they decide whether a 1.3 GW Jalapeno plan is a slide or a schedule. The same week as Broadcom’s print, HPE’s Q3 FY2026 disclosure made the complementary point from the systems side: revenue $12.2 billion (+34% YoY), record non-GAAP gross margin 40.4%, and management commentary that normalized orders grew about 42% while revenue grew 34%, with the eight-point gap attributed to DDR5, DDR4, NAND, and wafer supply, a constraint they expect into FY2027, against a $7.6 billion AI backlog. Separate analysis of optical components put 800G and 1.6T transceiver lead times past 40 weeks while ordinary 100G still ships in 8–14. Read together: Broadcom is selling the custom accelerator story; the industry’s conversion story is still gated by memory and photonics. That is not a contradiction. It is the same bottleneck migrating down the stack.

Sources for the adjacent supply picture: FourWeekMBA on HPE Q3 FY2026 and Markets Insider / StockAlpha on 40-week optical lead times.

Third, residual value guarantees change how I read lab CapEx claims. If a chip vendor is willing to share residual risk so a lab can take more silicon sooner, headline “capacity commitments” may embed financing structure as much as pure demand. That does not make the demand fake. It does mean you should ask, for any multi-gigawatt roadmap, who carries the economic risk if utilization or model economics miss.

How I read the soft guide

A $200–250 million miss versus consensus on a ~$35 billion quarter is not a demand collapse. Broadcom’s semiconductor line still tripled. The selloff pattern (beat, then soft near-term guide, while management paints a $115B then $230B AI revenue path) matches what I am seeing across AI infrastructure prints this season: the market has stopped paying a premium for “AI demand is huge” and started discounting anything that implies conversion will take longer than the slide deck.

Custom ASICs amplify that tension. Tape-outs, yield, package, and customer software bring-up all introduce schedule variance that a catalog GPU SKU partially amortizes across many buyers. Jalapeno gen-2 tape-out and Ironwood acceleration for Anthropic are exactly the kind of programs that can slip a quarter without changing the multi-year thesis. Soft Q4 guidance can be inventory timing, customer readiness, or foundry allocation. The call did not give a clean single cause. As a practitioner, I treat the near-term guide as a reminder that ASIC programs have fat tails on delivery even when the customer list is elite.

What I am watching next

Three concrete checks, not a thesis laundry list:

  1. Ship vs slide on Ironwood and Jalapeno. Do Anthropic’s TPU8i GW targets and OpenAI’s 1.3 GW 2027 Jalapeno plan show up as recognized revenue and deployed capacity on subsequent calls, or do they stretch?
  2. Guarantee structure. If residual value guarantees move from “considering” to signed, how are they sized, and do they show up as contingent liabilities that change how aggressive labs can be on order books?
  3. Memory and optics as the silent gates. If HPE-style order/revenue gaps and 40-week optical lead times persist, Broadcom’s AI doubling path still depends on someone else’s wafers, HBM, and InP lasers. The custom chip win does not remove that convoy problem.

I am not offering investment advice. I am reading a public earnings call the way I read a capacity plan: named customers, named silicon, named watts, and an honest look at which layer of the stack is allowed to be late. Broadcom’s Q3 says the custom ASIC lane for frontier labs is real, funded, and multi-generational, and that the Street will sell a beat if the next quarter’s conversion looks even slightly late. That combination is the story worth tracking.
