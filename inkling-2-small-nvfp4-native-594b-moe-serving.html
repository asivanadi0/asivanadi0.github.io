Inkling 2 Small Was Trained in NVFP4 From the Start: What It Takes to Serve a 594B Open MoE on Blackwell

14 November 2026 · Anpoo Sivanadi

Most open-weight MoE releases I've deployed this year arrive in BF16 or FP8, and FP4 shows up later as a post-training quantization that someone has to validate against the original. Inkling 2 Small from Thinking Machines Lab goes the other way. The model card says it was pretrained, midtrained and post-trained in NVFP4, so the 4-bit checkpoint is the model, not an approximation of it. The weights went up on Hugging Face under Apache 2.0 on Nov 12, a week after the closed-weights launch on Nov 5, with day-0 recipes in vLLM and SGLang.

I care less about the leaderboard position than about what this means for the people who have to run it. Below is what I pulled out of the launch post, the card, and the two serving recipes. All numbers are vendor or framework-maintainer numbers; I haven't run it myself yet.

1. The shape of the model

The card lists 594B total parameters and 17B active. The vLLM blog's architecture figure says 18B active, and so does Cloudflare's model page, so treat the active count as 17 to 18B until Thinking Machines clarifies what each figure includes.

From the vLLM blog's comparison table:

Inkling (July) Inkling 2 Small
Layers 66 (55 sliding-window + 11 full) 48 (40 sliding-window + 8 full)
Heads (query / KV) 64 / 8 full, 64 / 16 sliding 48 / 8 in all layers
Sliding window 512 1,024
MoE 256 routed (top-6) + 2 shared 384 routed (top-8), no shared, all layers MoE
Expert precision NVFP4 NVFP4 (layers 0-39), MXFP8 (layers 40-47)
Speculative decoding 8 chained MTP heads DSpark draft, 7 tokens per step

Two architectural choices matter for serving. Block attention residuals replace the plain residual stream: before every sublayer, a learned query mixes the outputs of earlier 6-layer blocks. And attention uses a learned relative bias instead of RoPE, with four short convolutions per layer. Both add memory-bound work at every sublayer boundary, which is exactly where the framework teams spent their effort.

2. How it was trained

The training section of the launch post is unusually specific:

  • Pretraining phase H1: 8.4 trillion tokens in 68 hours on GB300 NVL72 racks, natively in NVFP4. They credit the NVL72 domain for enabling high expert parallelism and promise a separate post on the NVFP4 recipe.
  • H2: 3.8 trillion tokens of newer, higher-quality data at 512K context.
  • H3: about 320 billion tokens of reasoning and agent trajectories at 1M context, plus replayed pretraining data.
  • H4: about 34 billion tokens of the highest-quality agent trajectories.
  • One joint RL run: 1,075 steps, over 17 million rollouts, 9 days, 2,048 GPUs, over 4 x 10^23 FLOPs, 3T+ tokens.

The RL systems details are what I'd steal for any long-horizon agent training setup. Rollouts ran p50 43K and p99 1.5M total tokens, p50 8 minutes and p99 4 hours. Median policy staleness was 17 updates with a rollout batch of 16K. To keep a 5:3 sampler-to-trainer ratio at long context they used sticky routing and DRAM KV-cache offload to hold a prefix cache hit rate above 93%, and moved weights from trainers to samplers over GPUDirect RDMA at over 93% of line rate, with a parallelism-invariant checksum to verify the copy.

They also report that midway through RL, their monitor caught the model working around a network block through a third-party service reachable from its search tools. They paused, removed environments that needed web access, added synchronous tool-call monitoring, and resumed. If you run agentic RL with live network access, that paragraph is worth reading in full.

3. What the checkpoint actually contains

"Trained in NVFP4" needs one qualifier. The released checkpoint stores routed experts in NVFP4 for layers 0 to 39 and MXFP8 for layers 40 to 47. Attention, routers, the attention-residual queries and the LM head stay in BF16 or FP32. The vLLM blog says the mixed-precision MoE is what lets the model be served on a single node.

On activations, NVFP4 uses a per-token global scale plus the Four Over Six block scaling trick: for each 16-element block, the quantizer tries scaling the block maximum to 6 and to 4 and keeps whichever has lower reconstruction error.

Sizes, from the two recipes:

  • Main checkpoint (NVFP4 + MXFP8): about 393 to 394 GB on disk, including a 3.2 GB DSpark draft. About 94 GB per GPU at TP4.
  • All-MXFP8 checkpoint: about 623 GB. About 151 GB per GPU at TP4 and about 311 GB per GPU at TP2.

The card's minimum is at least 500 GB of aggregate VRAM for the main checkpoint (2x B300 or 4x H200) and at least 800 GB for MXFP8 (4x B300 or 8x H200). Thinking Machines says the MXFP8 version gains only 1 to 2 points on evals and recommends NVFP4 for everything. Given that it was trained this way, I believe them more than I would for a post-hoc quant.

One constraint from the SGLang notes that will catch people: keep expert parallelism equal to tensor parallelism. The NVFP4 experts can't be TP-sharded, because their activation scales have to cover each expert's full input width. TP4 means TP4 attention with EP4 experts.

4. Serving it: what the recipes say

Context vs hardware. vLLM verified one GB200 tray at TP4 for contexts up to 262,144 tokens, and two trays at TP8 for the full 1,048,576. Its troubleshooting note says a single 1M-token request needs 64.47 GiB of KV cache and does not fit on one 4-GPU tray. SGLang says B200 and GB200 (192 GB) at TP4 hold about a third of GB300's KV pool, so long-context concurrency on those parts is limited unless you go TP8 on a full node.

Kernel work. Three items from the vLLM blog stood out:

  • FlashAttention-4's decode path silently disables GQA packing at this model's 6:1 query-to-KV ratio. A custom warp-specialized Gluon kernel packs the 6 heads into one 64-row tile and reads KV once. A batch-1 decode step at 100K context drops from 15.1 ms to 4.8 ms.
  • A fused allreduce_conv_mix kernel does the all-reduce over NVLink multicast and keeps the short conv, residual update, attention-residual mix and RMSNorm in registers.
  • Sequence parallelism for prefill cuts an 8K-token prefill step by 22% at TP4 and 26% at TP8, and frees 1.3 to 1.9 GiB per GPU for KV cache.

On the SGLang side, the MegaMoE backend fuses dispatch, both expert GEMMs, the SwiGLU and the combine into one DeepGEMM kernel for any forward with at least 64 tokens. On GB300 it cut median time to first token by 19 to 29% with no decode slowdown. Its per-rank token cap of 4,096 costs about 1.2% of the KV pool, against 6.2% at the default of 8,192.

Throughput. The vLLM agentic benchmark used TP8 across two 4-GPU nodes, DSpark with probabilistic drafting, prefix caching, about 3K new tokens per turn and 1,024 output tokens with reasoning on at temperature 1.0. Per-GPU output throughput at the concurrency that gives each user 100 tokens/s:

Session context Output tok/s per GPU Concurrent sessions Prefix-cache hit Acceptance length
32K ~1,070 ~84 91% 3.3
64K ~915 ~72 95% 3.4
128K ~700 ~55 98% 3.5
256K ~450 ~37 99% 3.5

By my arithmetic, going from 32K to 256K sessions cuts per-GPU throughput by about 58% even though the cache hit rate climbs to 99%. Capacity planning for agent workloads has to be done against session length, not request rate. A single user at TP4 decodes at about 630 tokens/s with an acceptance length of 3.5.

One benchmarking trap the recipe calls out: DSpark acceptance depends on prompts and temperature. Random-token prompts accept almost nothing, and vLLM's default greedy drafting accepts about 15 to 25% less at temperature 1.0 than the probabilistic setting above.

Non-NVIDIA. SGLang has recipes for MI350X and MI355X (validated on gfx950 only; other ROCm GPUs refuse at launch) and for a 4-chip TPU7x host at TP8/EP8. On AMD, note that the ROCm image enables ROCM_QUICK_REDUCE_QUANTIZATION=INT8, a lossy all-reduce, and the recipe sets it to NONE. If you bring your own launch script, check that variable.

5. Capability, briefly

On the card's own table, Inkling 2 Small scores 30.6 on the Artificial Analysis Index v5.0, between GLM-5.3-Flash (27.8) and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 (32.6). It trails DeepSeek on DeepSWE (72.1% vs 77.0%) and leads it on BrowseComp with compaction (90.2% vs 87.8%) and AA-LCR (83.3% vs 82.7%). These are vendor-run comparisons at effort 0.99, temperature 1.0 and a 1M-token trajectory limit, so I'd treat them as a rough placement, not a ranking.

6. What I'd take from it

  • Native FP4 training is now a shipped, open fact, not a paper result. For operators, it mostly removes the "did quantization hurt it" question for the main checkpoint. The launch post doesn't say whether the MXFP8 layers 40 to 47 were also trained that way, so that's the part I'd want the recipe post to explain.
  • One Blackwell tray is enough for the weights, not for the context. Budget two trays per replica if you actually need 1M tokens.
  • Don't TP-shard the experts. EP must equal TP for the NVFP4 layers.
  • Benchmark with realistic prompts and probabilistic drafting, or your speculative decoding numbers will be misleadingly low.

What's missing: no independent throughput numbers yet, SGLang's benchmark table still says data pending, the NVFP4 training recipe post hasn't been published, and the tool-calling parser is unit-tested but, per the vLLM recipe, has no end-to-end benchmark yet.

For a comparison with a checkpoint that was quantized to NVFP4 after training, see my notes on the DeepSeek V4.1 Flash NVFP4 checkpoint.
