LTX-2.5 on Trainium3: 22 Seconds per Clip, and Most of the Win Was on the Host

28 November 2026 · Anpoo Sivanadi

On November 27 Hugging Face published Taking Trainium3 to the Track, a write-up by David Corvoysier, Jingya Huang and Sayak Paul. They had a trn3.48xlarge (64 NeuronCores) for a month, and in the last five days Corvoysier ported LTX-2.5, Lightricks' 19B model that generates video and sound together. The headline: once the models are loaded, a 10-second clip with sound at 2048x1024 takes 22 to 23 seconds on 32 NeuronCores, half the machine.

Most new-accelerator posts give you a chart. This one gives you two tables of problems, workarounds and measured gains, with links to the code and upstream issues. I wanted to know where the time actually went.

1. The before and after

The post's own summary:

10-second clip with sound NeuronCores Wall time On the NeuronCores
First attempt, 960x544 4 5 min 20 s 45 s
Optimized, 2048x1024 32 22 to 23 s 15 s

The first attempt was the model card's pipeline with the transformer split over 4 cores. The cores were busy for 45 of its 320 seconds.

My arithmetic: the device share of wall time went from about 14% to about 67%. Host time per clip fell from about 280 s to about 8 s (the post says ~35x), while device time fell from 45 s to 15 s at four times the pixels (960x544 is 0.52 megapixels, 2048x1024 is 2.10). In core-seconds per megapixel, the optimized run is about 7x cheaper than the first. In raw device core-seconds it costs more: 32 x 15 = 480 against 4 x 45 = 180, from the extra pixels, the second stage the first attempt could not load, and spreading one clip over 32 cores.

My read: the 14x is mostly a host story.

2. The host fixes

These are the rows I would check first on any accelerator port:

  • Every rank ran the whole pipeline. One process per NeuronCore meant 32 copies of prompt encoding, all on the CPUs of one socket. Running the pipeline on rank 0 only and sending the other ranks the arguments for each call took prompt encoding from about 205 s to 7.5 s.
  • Thread pool starvation. Rank 0 runs the CPU stages (text encoder, upsampler, vocoder), but every rank got a node-wide torch thread pool. Pinning rank 0's threads to CPUs no other rank uses, and capping the rest to a fair share, took prompt encoding from 7.4 s to 3.3 s and the stage 1 step from 0.9 s to 0.6 s.
  • Encoding padding. The pipeline pads prompts to 1,024 tokens, and the CPU text encoder was encoding the padding. Encoding only the real tokens took it from 3.4 s to 1.4 s.
  • Video decode on CPU. Decode at 2048x1024 took 357 s on CPU, and more cores did not help. Moving it to the NeuronCores, split by frames and handed back to rank 0 through shared memory, took it to 1.6 s.

My take: none of these are exotic. They would hurt on a GPU box too. On a new device you just tend to blame the device first.

3. The device fixes, and the TP-4 x SP-8 layout

The transformer does not fit on one NeuronCore, and diffusers has no tensor-parallel plan for LTX-2.5. So the post writes one, with an attention processor that runs on one rank's heads. Tensor parallelism is forced by memory and capped by the model's 32 attention heads. At 2048x1024 the 4-way block graph failed to load, so they added 8-way sequence parallelism on top, splitting video tokens across ranks with two custom attention processors on diffusers' set_attn_processor hook. The diagram caption puts the TP split inside a chip and the SP split across chips. That is how one clip uses 32 cores, and stage 2 runs at 3.0 s per step.

The other device rows:

  • Compile one block, not the whole graph. As one graph, the transformer returned NaN at one size and retraced in every new process. compile_repeated_blocks gave finite outputs and cut tracing from 388 s to 18 s.
  • bf16 GELU. torch-neuronx computed a bf16 GELU entirely in bf16, constants included. Doing GELUs in fp32 cut transformer error against fp32 from 0.297 to 0.158, against 0.160 for CPU bf16.
  • Allocator waste. The allocator left most of its segments unused. Packing parameters into buffers of at most 1 GiB took memory per core from 22.96 GiB to 10.2 GiB, which by my arithmetic is 56% less.
  • Attention shape rule. Attention fell back to plain ops unless the sequence length was a multiple of 128 tokens. Generating stage 1 at 1024x512 (512 tokens per frame) with NEURON_LOGICAL_NC_CONFIG=2 took the transformer step from 4.3 s to 3.0 s.
  • Strided copies. A strided slice copied to the device was filled by a slow on-device copy. Calling .contiguous() first took a 16-core step from 5.6 s to 1.0 s.

A check on the numbers, my arithmetic: the recipe is 8 distilled steps at stage 1, a 2x latent upsample, then 3 steps at stage 2. At 0.6 s and 3.0 s per step that is 4.8 + 9.0 = 13.8 s of transformer time, which fits inside the post's ~15 s on the NeuronCores. And if 1024x512 gives 512 tokens per frame, each token covers 32x32 pixels, so stage 2 at 2048x1024 has 2,048 tokens per frame. Both are multiples of 128.

My read: the post's own line, "plan for the size of the graphs", is the lesson. On Trainium, shape decides whether attention takes the fast kernel, and graph size decides whether a graph loads at all. Sequence parallelism here was less a speed trick than a way to make each core's graph small enough to load.

4. Where the toolchain still is

I checked the linked upstream issues on November 28:

  • torch-neuronx #1027, a NEFF cache key that depended on where the Python files were installed, is closed. The post says edits to traced code could trigger recompiles of up to 30 minutes per graph in the build it used.
  • #1049 (bf16 GELU accuracy), #1050 (slow non-contiguous copy) and #1052 (allocator segment space) are all open. Filed November 25.
  • diffusers #15420 (LTX-2 device placement for split-device pipelines) and diffusers #14725 (tensor and context parallelism in one ParallelConfig) are both open.

So today you need the workarounds in dacorvo/video-generation. Three problems stayed unexplained: the NaN at one size, a slow first keyframe-conditioned shot in each new process even with a warm cache, and a latent upsampler that will not load next to the transformer, so it stays on the CPU.

When I wrote about the TorchNeuron public beta in October, I said the operator questions would be compile behavior, caches and shapes. This post is a list of exactly those.

5. What it means if you would serve this

From the post: processes take about 3 minutes to load and 80 s for the first clip, and the model often gets details wrong, so the author made 4 takes of each new shot.

My arithmetic: a cold start of about 260 s is worth about 11 warm clips, so this only makes sense with long-lived processes. Four takes at 22.5 s is about 90 s of 32-core time per kept 10-second shot. The post does not give an instance price, so I cannot turn that into dollars, and the Trainium3 AgentX numbers do not carry over to a diffusion workload.

On comparisons, the post is careful. It cites LTX's online API at 23.7 s for a 10-second 1080p clip. My arithmetic: 2048x1024 is about 1% more pixels than 1920x1080, so 22 to 23 s is on par, not a clear win. VentureBeat's report frames its comparison around 720p, so treat the API resolution as unsettled. LTX's 6.8 s figure comes from self-hosting on two GB200 superchips (four B200 GPUs), and the author set it aside because the method is not stated. I agree with that call.

Two clips at once on all 64 cores is the obvious next step. The post does not measure it, and given the thread pinning rank 0 already needed, I would not assume it doubles throughput.

6. What this does not show

  • No measured CUDA baseline on the same pipeline, so there is no like-for-like GPU comparison.
  • No cost per clip, because no instance price is given.
  • One engineer, five days. Several fixes work around open issues.
  • No batching or multi-clip concurrency numbers.
  • I have not run any of this.

My take

The useful finding is not that Trainium3 can generate video. It is that the first working port kept the NeuronCores idle for 86% of its wall time, and the host fixes were ordinary: one pipeline owner instead of 32, pinned threads, no padding work, decode on the device. The real Neuron lessons are on the device side, where graph size and shape rules decide what loads and what runs fast. If you are evaluating a new accelerator for a diffusion model, read this post's two fix tables before any benchmark.
