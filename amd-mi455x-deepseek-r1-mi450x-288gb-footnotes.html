AMD's 21,333 Tokens per Second on MI455X and the 288 GB MI450X, Read Against the Footnotes

13 November 2026 · Anpoo Sivanadi

On November 12 AMD posted an MI400 Series update that packs three things into one page: a new DeepSeek-R1 throughput number for MI455X on the Helios rack, a new lower-memory part called MI450X, and more detail on the FP64-heavy MI430X. The headline is "up to 30% higher throughput than Nvidia Vera Rubin." The part I'd actually plan around is the MI450X, and the part I'd push back on is how the 30% was measured.

I've written about Helios and its UALink-over-Ethernet scale-up, the Vera Rubin NVL72 MLPerf v6.1 preview, and NVIDIA's revised Rubin HBM4 and NVLink numbers. This post leans on all three, because the new claims only make sense next to them.

1. What AMD published

From the newsroom post and the two product pages (MI455X, MI450X):

MI455X MI450X
Architecture CDNA 5, TSMC 2 nm and 3 nm same
Work group processors 256 256
Peak MXFP4 40.3 PFLOPS 40.3 PFLOPS
Peak FP8 / MXFP8 20.1 PFLOPS 20.1 PFLOPS
Peak matrix FP64 5 TFLOPS 5 TFLOPS
HBM4 capacity 432 GB, 12 stacks 288 GB, 8 stacks
Peak memory bandwidth 23.3 TB/s 23.3 TB/s
L2 cache 192 MB 192 MB
Scale-up (UALoE, bidirectional) 3.6 TB/s 3.6 TB/s
Scale-out (bidirectional) 600 GB/s 600 GB/s
Form factor, cooling EAM, direct liquid EAM, direct liquid

So MI450X is the same 320-billion-transistor part with a third less memory. AMD's wording: "for workloads that fit within its memory capacity," it meets throughput and response-time targets "while optimizing operating expenses." AMD gives no price, no power difference and no savings figure.

The inference claim, from footnote 1: AMD Performance Labs measured 21,333 tokens per second per GPU on DeepSeek-R1 "on an AMD-tested MLPerf Inference Offline configuration" in October 2026, compared with NVIDIA's published MLPerf Inference v6.1 Closed result for Vera Rubin NVL72 (entry 6.1-0106).

2. The 30% is AMD's own run against a peer-reviewed one

MLPerf results are worth something because MLCommons reviews them. An "AMD-tested MLPerf Inference Offline configuration" means AMD ran the benchmark harness itself. It wasn't submitted to MLCommons and nobody outside AMD has checked it. The NVIDIA side of the comparison went through review, as a preview-category entry. These are different kinds of evidence, and the footnote says so if you read it.

There are other gaps too. The post doesn't say how many MI455X GPUs were in the run, whether it was a full 72-GPU Helios rack or a single tray, what precision was used, or which serving stack ran it. NVIDIA's MLPerf blog says its Rubin DeepSeek-R1 runs used TensorRT-LLM with disaggregated prefill and decode and large-scale expert parallelism. If AMD used a different scheme, per-GPU throughput is not an apples-to-apples unit.

Offline is also the easiest scenario. It's a pure batch throughput test with no latency limit. Nebius published a Server-scenario DeepSeek-R1 number for its Rubin preview system (591,368 tokens per second), and AMD compared only on Offline. If you run interactive traffic, the Server and Interactive results are the ones you'd want to see.

For scale, Nebius's v6.1 results list a separate Vera Rubin preview entry (6.1-0107) at 558,803 tokens per second offline on 36 Rubin GPUs. That works out to about 15,522 per GPU (my arithmetic). Taking AMD's "up to 30%" literally puts NVIDIA's own entry near 16,400 per GPU. I haven't confirmed that number on the MLCommons results page, so treat it as implied rather than published.

None of this makes 21,333 wrong. It makes it a vendor number. The fix is cheap: submit it in the next MLPerf round.

3. The bandwidth lead grew because NVIDIA's number shrank

Footnote 2 says Helios has "up to 20% higher HBM bandwidth" than Vera Rubin, based on Rubin's "current memory bandwidth specifications." That word "current" matters.

In September, AMD's Helios footnotes claimed about 6% more HBM bandwidth than Rubin. Since then NVIDIA's NVL72 page has cut Rubin from 22 TB/s to 19.2 TB/s per GPU, and NVLink from 3.6 TB/s to 3 TB/s. Against the new figures, 23.3 over 19.2 is 1.21, and 3.6 over 3.0 is 1.2. MI455X didn't get faster. The comparison point moved.

One oddity: the comparison chart on AMD's own MI455X product page still shows Vera Rubin at 22.0 TB/s next to MI455X's 23.3 TB/s. The newsroom footnote and the product page are using different Rubin numbers. Anyone building a slide off either one should pick one and cite it.

4. MI450X: the capacity math

This is where I'd spend my planning time. A 288 GB MI450X has the same HBM4 capacity as a Rubin GPU, while keeping MI455X's compute and bandwidth. Using the 72-GPU Helios rack from AMD's earlier materials, the rack drops from about 31 TB of HBM4 to about 20.7 TB (72 × 288 GB, my arithmetic).

For DeepSeek-R1, MXFP4 stores an 8-bit scale per 32 values, so it's about 4.25 bits per parameter. At 671 billion parameters that's roughly 356 GB of weights. That doesn't fit on one MI450X but does fit on one MI455X with about 75 GB to spare. Nobody serves R1 on a single GPU at scale, though. With expert parallelism across a tray or a rack, the weights are a small slice of the 20.7 TB, and what's left goes to KV cache. KV cache is the thing that scales with context length and concurrency, and that's exactly where AMD says MI455X "provides additional memory headroom."

A rough way to decide:

  • If your model fits with room to spare and your contexts are short to medium (chat, agent tool calls, most code completion), the MI450X gives up capacity you aren't using, at the same bandwidth and compute.
  • If you run long contexts at high concurrency, or you keep large prefix caches warm, the extra 144 GB per GPU on MI455X is the whole point. Running out of KV cache space means smaller batches or recomputing prefixes, and both cost you more than the memory would.
  • If you're planning one fleet for several models, buy for the biggest model's KV cache footprint, not its weights.

The question I'd ask AMD first: the MI450X has 8 HBM4 stacks against MI455X's 12, at the same 23.3 TB/s. That's about 2.9 TB/s per stack versus about 1.9 TB/s. On a 2,048-bit HBM4 interface, 2.9 TB/s needs roughly 11.4 Gb/s per pin, well above JEDEC HBM4's 8 Gb/s baseline, while 1.9 TB/s is about 7.6 Gb/s. Either the 8-stack part runs its memory much faster, or the 23.3 TB/s on the MI450X page is a copy of the MI455X row. I'd want that answered before sizing anything on it, because for decode, bandwidth is the spec that actually sets your tokens per second.

5. MI430X and the FP64 split

The same post adds detail on MI430X: native FP64, 432 GB of HBM4, up to 23.3 TB/s. AMD cites internal research across 47 configurations of 11 applications showing where FP64 emulation helps and where codes need native double precision. That fits with the Ozaki emulation paper from AMD authors I wrote up this week. The product split is now explicit. MI455X and MI450X list 5 TFLOPS of matrix FP64, which is a token amount, and MI430X is the part for simulation. If you run mixed HPC and AI clusters, don't assume the AI SKU will run your FP64 codes.

What I'd do with this

  • Treat 21,333 tokens per second per GPU as an AMD lab result until it shows up on the MLCommons results page, ideally in the Server scenario as well as Offline.
  • When you compare bandwidth, write down which Rubin number you used, 22 or 19.2 TB/s, because AMD's own pages don't agree.
  • Look at MI450X seriously if your models fit in 288 GB with KV cache headroom. Get the per-stack bandwidth confirmed first.
  • Keep FP64 work on MI430X or on CPUs. The AI parts aren't built for it.
